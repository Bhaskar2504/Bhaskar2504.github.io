Topic Area

Engineering Data & Power/Process

Turning raw industrial measurements into trustworthy engineering information requires much more than collecting tags: timestamps, context, units, system boundaries, uncertainty and physics all matter.

← Back to Insights

Industrial analytics succeeds or fails on the quality of the engineering data model underneath it. A tag name alone does not tell us what a signal means, where it is measured, whether the unit is correct, whether the equipment was operating, or whether two measurements represent the same time.

The purpose of engineering data work is to make those assumptions explicit before calculations, models or diagnostics are trusted.

Core principle

Context gives data meaning.

Pressure, temperature, current or flow becomes useful only when its equipment boundary, measurement location, unit, operating state and relationship to other signals are understood.

01

Industrial time-series data

Sampling and resampling

Plant data may arrive at sub-second, second, minute or irregular intervals. Converting it to a common interval requires an explicit rule—mean, min, max, last value or another engineering aggregation—because each preserves different information.

Time alignment

Two tags that appear in the same row may still represent different physical moments if historian timestamps, communication delays or aggregation windows are inconsistent. Relationship-based diagnostics depend on synchronized data.

Missing data

Missing values should not be filled automatically without considering duration, operating state and signal behaviour. Short gaps in a slow-changing signal are different from long gaps during a transient.

Operating context

Startup, shutdown, equipment changeover, low load and control-mode transitions can dominate the data distribution. A model trained without context may learn operating transitions rather than equipment health.

02

Sensor quality and uncertainty

A measurement is not the true physical value; it is an estimate produced by a sensor, installation, transmitter, conversion chain and data system.

Common data-quality problems

Flat-lining, spikes, dropouts, frozen values, impossible rates of change, unit errors, swapped tags, drift and inconsistent peer measurements can all create false engineering conclusions.

Accuracy vs uncertainty

Accuracy is usually a specification about closeness to a reference. Measurement uncertainty represents the credible range around a reported value considering calibration, resolution, repeatability, drift and other contributors.

Derived KPI uncertainty

When several measured variables are combined in a calculation, their uncertainties propagate into the calculated result. A small change in a KPI should not automatically be treated as meaningful if it is comparable to measurement and process variability.

Peer and physics checks

Redundant sensors, mass/energy relationships and related process variables can help distinguish real process movement from an instrument problem.

03

Tag mapping and calculated KPIs

Semantic mapping

A useful tag map connects the historian signal to plant → system → equipment → measurement point → parameter type → unit. This makes the signal reusable across analytics rather than meaningful only to one person.

Unit and type validation

Pressure can be absolute or gauge; temperature may be °C or K; flow may be mass or volumetric; current may represent one phase or an average. Calculations are only correct when the measurement definition is correct.

System boundary

Before calculating performance, define exactly what enters and leaves the boundary. Pump hydraulic power, turbine efficiency or heat-exchanger effectiveness can be wrong even with accurate sensors if the boundary is wrong.

KPI validation

A calculated KPI should be checked against dimensional consistency, expected operating range, known reference conditions, independent calculations and plant behaviour before it is used for alerting or optimization.

04

Pumps, motors, boilers and turbines

Equipment analytics becomes useful when measurements are interpreted through the physics and operating relationships of the asset.

Pumps

Flow, suction pressure, discharge pressure, speed, valve position and motor electrical signals should be interpreted together. A changing operating point may alter flow, head and current even when the pump itself is healthy.

Motors

Current alone is not motor power. Three-phase electrical power depends on voltage, current and power factor, with efficiency required when converting electrical input to shaft output. A common bus power factor should not automatically be assigned to an individual motor.

Boilers / HRSG

Combustion, heat transfer, excess air, gas-side temperatures, pressure drops, steam conditions and feedwater conditions interact. Performance deterioration often appears as a coordinated pattern rather than one abnormal tag.

Steam turbines

Steam pressure, temperature, flow, exhaust conditions, load, valve position and vibration together provide operating and mechanical context. Changes in efficiency or vibration should be interpreted against load and steam conditions.

05

CCGT and plant performance

Performance is condition-dependent

Ambient temperature, humidity, fuel conditions, condenser pressure, equipment cleanliness and operating configuration can materially affect output and efficiency. Raw performance values should not be compared without context.

Mass and energy balance

Plant KPIs are strongest when they are connected to conservation laws. Fuel input, electrical output, steam generation, heat rejection and auxiliary consumption should reconcile within a reasonable engineering tolerance.

Component → plant impact

A condenser, compressor, turbine, HRSG or auxiliary can degrade locally while the plant remains online. Linking component KPIs to plant heat rate, output or auxiliary consumption helps prioritize what matters.

Reference and normalization

Performance should often be compared with a reference curve, corrected condition or expected model rather than with one fixed historical number. The comparison method must be transparent so deviations remain interpretable.

Thermodynamic Performance

From component KPIs to a whole-cycle diagnosis.

Steam-cycle performance is a connected problem: boiler heat input, turbine expansion, condenser heat rejection and regenerative feedwater heating continuously influence one another.

Cycle efficiency

Use the Rankine-cycle energy balance to connect heat input, turbine work, pump work and condenser heat rejection instead of treating performance indicators independently.

Boiler losses & excess air

Dry flue-gas loss, moisture, unburnt carbon and radiation losses create competing optimization targets. O₂ should be interpreted with CO, gas temperature and air-ingress context.

Turbine & condenser diagnostics

Stage-pressure relationships, isentropic heat drop, condenser TTD, cooling-water conditions and air ingress help convert a heat-rate deviation into testable engineering hypotheses.

Feedwater-heater cascade

TTD and DCA help identify heater degradation, while a bypassed heater can increase boiler duty and alter extraction and condenser conditions elsewhere in the cycle.

Data-to-Decision Flow

What has to happen before analytics can be trusted.

1. Identify

Know what each signal physically represents and where it is measured.

2. Validate

Check unit, type, range, timestamp, quality and operating availability.

3. Contextualize

Attach asset hierarchy, operating mode, load and system configuration.

4. Calculate

Create engineering features and KPIs using defined equations and boundaries.

5. Compare

Compare measured behaviour with expected, reference or physically consistent behaviour.

6. Interpret

Convert the deviation into a testable engineering question rather than an automatic conclusion.
