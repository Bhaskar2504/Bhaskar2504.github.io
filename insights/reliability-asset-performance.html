Topic Area

Reliability & Asset Performance

How failure mechanisms, evidence, maintenance strategy and asset-performance context come together to support better engineering decisions.

← Back to Insights

Reliability work begins with a simple question: How can an asset fail, how would that failure reveal itself, and what should we do before the consequence becomes unacceptable?

Good diagnostics and Asset Performance Management connect this reliability logic to real operating data, inspection evidence, maintenance history and business consequence.

Core principle

Symptoms are not failure modes.

High vibration, low pressure or high temperature describe observations. Reliability reasoning asks what mechanisms could produce those observations, what evidence supports each mechanism and what evidence would contradict it.

01

FMEA and RCA

FMEA is forward-looking

Failure Mode and Effects Analysis asks what can fail, why it can fail, what the effect would be and how the failure might be detected or prevented. It is useful for designing monitoring, inspection and maintenance strategies before an event occurs.

RCA is evidence-led

Root Cause Analysis begins after a problem or event and works backward from evidence. The goal is not to choose the first plausible explanation but to test causal hypotheses against timelines, operating conditions, physical evidence and known failure mechanisms.

Failure mode vs cause

A bearing may fail by fatigue, overheating, lubrication loss, contamination or misalignment-related damage. “Bearing high temperature” is a symptom; “lubrication loss” may be a cause; the physical damage mechanism is the failure mode.

Where AI can help

AI can retrieve relevant failure knowledge, organize evidence and rank hypotheses, but the FMEA structure and engineering validation should remain visible so the system does not turn correlation into causation.

02

Failure signatures

A failure signature is the characteristic combination of measurements, trends and relationships expected when a particular mechanism develops.

Single-tag signatures are weak

One abnormal signal can have many causes. Low flow, for example, may result from restriction, low speed, valve position, recirculation, process demand, sensor error or a different system configuration.

Relationships create specificity

Combining suction pressure, discharge pressure, flow, valve position, motor current and operating state can make a pump diagnosis far more discriminating than any one signal alone.

Contradicting evidence matters

If a suspected failure should cause several related changes but only one measurement moves, that disagreement should reduce confidence and may shift attention toward instrumentation or context.

Persistence matters

Industrial processes contain transients and noise. A credible signature usually requires persistence, repeated occurrence or a consistent relationship with operating conditions rather than a single instantaneous deviation.

03

Equipment diagnostics

Diagnostics is the process of moving from observed behaviour to a justified engineering hypothesis.

Mechanical

Vibration amplitude, phase, spectrum, bearing temperature, speed and load can support hypotheses such as unbalance, misalignment, looseness, rub or bearing deterioration. The pattern and operating dependency matter as much as absolute magnitude.

Hydraulic & process

Pressure, flow, differential pressure, level, valve position and speed help distinguish equipment degradation from changes elsewhere in the system boundary.

Thermal

Temperature approaches, heat balance, pressure, flow and gas or fluid composition can reveal changes in heat transfer, leakage, fouling or operating efficiency.

Electrical & instrumentation

Voltage, current, power, power factor, temperatures and peer-sensor agreement help separate electrical loading problems from process loading or measurement faults.

Diagnostic discipline: define the system boundary first. A pump problem may actually originate in the suction system, discharge system, control logic, process demand or instrumentation.

04

Reliability engineering

Risk combines probability and consequence

Reliability decisions should consider not only whether failure is likely but also what the consequence would be for safety, production, environment, cost or equipment damage.

Maintenance strategy should match failure behaviour

Condition-based maintenance is useful when deterioration can be detected with enough lead time. Time-based maintenance is appropriate for some age-related failure modes. Run-to-failure may be acceptable for low-consequence assets.

Detectability matters

A failure mode is a good candidate for predictive monitoring only when available measurements contain a detectable precursor that appears early enough to support useful action.

Reliability is a feedback loop

Inspection results, confirmed failures, false alerts and maintenance findings should improve future failure-mode definitions, monitoring logic and diagnostic confidence.

05

Asset Performance Management

APM connects reliability, maintenance, performance, operations and risk. The goal is not simply to monitor assets—it is to improve decisions over the asset lifecycle.

Asset hierarchy and context

Good APM starts with clear plant, system, equipment and component boundaries. Signals, failure modes, work history and KPIs need to be connected to the correct asset context.

Health is multidimensional

Asset health may combine condition indicators, failure risk, process performance, operating severity, maintenance history and data quality. A single score is useful only if the underlying evidence remains visible.

Reliability and performance interact

An asset can remain available while gradually consuming more energy, losing capacity or operating farther from its preferred region. Performance deterioration can therefore become an early reliability signal.

From alert to workflow

The real value appears when an alert leads to review, evidence collection, engineering interpretation, maintenance planning, confirmation and feedback—not when it merely adds another notification.

A Practical Reliability Flow

From observation to action.

1. Observe

Identify the abnormal trend, relationship or event and verify the data is credible.

2. Frame

Define the asset, operating state, system boundary and consequence of the problem.

3. Hypothesize

List plausible failure mechanisms using FMEA, physics and equipment knowledge.

4. Test evidence

Check supporting and contradicting signals and decide what additional evidence is required.

5. Act

Recommend inspection, monitoring, maintenance or operational review appropriate to the confidence and consequence.

6. Learn

Use the confirmed outcome to improve the diagnostic logic and future monitoring.
