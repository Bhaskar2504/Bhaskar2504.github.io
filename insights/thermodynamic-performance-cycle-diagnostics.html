Thermodynamic Performance & Cycle Diagnostics

A practical view of how boiler, turbine, condenser and regenerative feedwater heating interact—and why plant performance should be diagnosed as one connected thermodynamic system.

← Back to Engineering Data & Power/Process

A steam-cycle plant does not contain isolated efficiency problems. A change in one component can shift pressure, temperature, extraction flow, cooling demand, auxiliary power and fuel demand elsewhere in the cycle.

The useful diagnostic question is therefore not only “Which parameter moved?” but “How did that movement propagate through the thermodynamic loop?”

Core principle

Optimize the cycle, not a single parameter.

Higher vacuum, more excess air, more spray, or bypassing a heater may solve a local operating problem while increasing loss somewhere else. The plant-level effect matters.

01

The steam cycle as an energy balance

The simplified Rankine-cycle view connects heat addition, turbine work, pump work and condenser heat rejection.

Net thermal efficiency:

η = (WT − WP) / Qin

η = net thermal efficiency of the cycle
WT = work produced by the steam turbine
WP = work consumed by the feedwater/condensate pumping process
Qin = heat supplied to the working fluid in the boiler and associated heat-addition stages

Boiler

Adds heat to feedwater and steam. The engineering objective is not simply maximum firing; it is high heat-transfer effectiveness with controlled combustion and minimum avoidable heat loss.

Turbine

Converts steam enthalpy drop into shaft work. Internal leakage, erosion, deposit build-up and changing stage pressure relationships reduce the useful work recovered from the same steam conditions.

Condenser

Rejects heat and establishes turbine exhaust pressure. Lower back pressure can improve turbine work, but cooling-water pumping and cooling-system constraints create a plant-level trade-off.

Feedwater heating

Uses extraction steam to raise feedwater temperature before it returns to the boiler. This sacrifices some turbine work locally but reduces the heat that the boiler must add.

02

How the practical Rankine cycle is improved

Superheating

Raises steam temperature before expansion, increasing the available heat-drop potential and helping limit excessive moisture during later expansion stages.

Reheating

Returns partially expanded steam for additional heat input before later turbine expansion. The purpose is both thermodynamic—recovering additional work—and mechanical—controlling exhaust wetness.

Regeneration

Uses extraction steam to preheat feedwater. This increases the average temperature at which heat is added to the cycle and reduces boiler heat input for a given feedwater condition.

Supercritical operation

At pressures above the critical point, the working fluid does not pass through the familiar subcritical boiling plateau. The process becomes more sensitive to coordinated feedwater, firing and temperature control.

03

Boiler efficiency: diagnose the losses

A useful boiler-performance view is to start with fuel energy and account for the routes by which useful heat is not transferred to steam.

Dry flue-gas loss

Energy leaves with hot dry combustion gas. Higher exit-gas temperature or unnecessary excess air can increase this loss.

Moisture / wet-gas loss

Energy is consumed in heating and carrying water vapour through the boiler and stack. Fuel moisture and hydrogen-derived water contribute to this term.

Unburnt-carbon / combustion loss

Incomplete combustion leaves chemical energy unrecovered. Low effective combustion air, poor mixing or combustion problems can increase this loss.

Radiation and unaccounted losses

Heat also leaves through surfaces, casing and smaller unmeasured paths. Loss-based efficiency methods help separate these mechanisms rather than hiding them inside one gross efficiency number.

Excess-air trade-off: too little effective air can increase CO and unburnt losses; too much air increases the mass of hot gas leaving the stack. Air ingress can also raise measured oxygen without contributing useful combustion air, so O₂ alone should not be interpreted without process context.

04

Turbine performance: use heat drop and pressure relationships

Isentropic efficiency:

ηiso = Actual heat drop / Isentropic heat drop

ηiso = turbine isentropic efficiency
Actual heat drop = actual decrease in steam enthalpy between turbine inlet and measured outlet condition
Isentropic heat drop = ideal enthalpy decrease for expansion to the same outlet pressure with no entropy increase

Internal leakage

Seal, gland or diaphragm leakage allows steam to bypass intended working stages, reducing useful expansion through the blades.

Blade/nozzle deterioration

Erosion, deposits and profile changes alter the flow path and can reduce stage efficiency or shift pressure distribution.

Stage-pressure survey

Plotting stage pressure against turbine output provides a baseline relationship. A broad parallel shift can suggest a distributed flow-path change, while a localized kink can focus investigation around a specific stage or restriction.

Trend the relationship, not only the value

Pressure, load, steam flow and temperature should be reviewed together. A stage-pressure deviation becomes more meaningful when it persists at comparable operating conditions.

05

Condenser performance: vacuum must be optimized

Condenser terminal temperature difference:

TTDcond = Tsat − TCW,out

TTDcond = condenser terminal temperature difference
Tsat = saturation temperature corresponding to condenser pressure
TCW,out = cooling-water outlet temperature

Tube fouling / scaling

Degraded heat transfer can increase condenser approach/TTD. Cooling-water temperature rise and flow context help distinguish fouling from other causes.

Low cooling-water flow

Insufficient flow reduces heat-removal capacity. Pump condition, valve position, strainers and flow-path restrictions should be checked before assuming a condenser-surface problem.

Air ingress

Non-condensable gas reduces effective heat transfer and worsens vacuum. Air-ejector/vacuum-system behaviour and leak checks provide supporting evidence.

High cooling-water inlet temperature

Warm inlet water reduces the available temperature driving force even if condenser tubes and water flow are healthy.

Plant-level trade-off: additional cooling-water pumping can improve condenser pressure, but the recovered turbine output should be compared with the extra auxiliary power required. “Maximum vacuum” is not automatically the economic optimum.

06

Regenerative feedwater heating: TTD and DCA as diagnostics

Terminal Temperature Difference (TTD)

TTD = Tshell,sat − TFW,out

Tshell,sat = saturation temperature corresponding to feedwater-heater shell pressure.
TFW,out = feedwater outlet temperature.

TTD indicates how closely the feedwater outlet approaches the condensing-steam temperature and is therefore a useful heat-transfer indicator.

Drain Cooler Approach (DCA)

DCA = Tdrain,out − TFW,in

Tdrain,out = heater drain outlet temperature.
TFW,in = feedwater inlet temperature.

DCA helps diagnose drain cooling, liquid level and internal drainage/baffle behaviour.

Common degradation patterns

Air accumulation can blank heat-transfer surface; steam-side or water-side fouling can increase thermal resistance; drainage defects can disturb level and subcooling behaviour.

Bypass creates a cascade

If a high-pressure heater is bypassed, colder feedwater returns to the boiler. Boiler heat input rises, turbine extraction conditions change and condenser heat rejection can also shift. The local heater issue becomes a cycle-wide performance issue.

07

Air–gas path and ESP performance are thermodynamically connected

Flue-gas velocity

Gas velocity influences residence time through heat-transfer and particulate-control equipment. Changes in air flow, leakage or gas temperature can therefore affect more than boiler efficiency alone.

Ash resistivity

Electrostatic precipitation performance depends on ash properties as well as electrical conditions. Coal chemistry, sulphur and moisture can influence collection behaviour.

Field shorting / hopper condition

Accumulated ash and mechanical problems can disturb electrical fields and collection performance. These are equipment-condition issues, not only stack-emission observations.

Thermal interaction

Air-heater leakage or higher flue-gas exit temperature can increase gas volume and velocity, creating a link between thermal performance and downstream ESP operating conditions.

08

The whole-plant diagnostic loop

1. Detect the local deviation

Examples: condenser pressure rises, feedwater-heater TTD changes, turbine stage pressure shifts, or flue-gas exit temperature increases.

2. Trace the thermodynamic response

Check what happens to heat transfer, extraction flow, boiler heat input, cooling demand, auxiliary power and net output.

3. Separate cause from consequence

A colder feedwater return may be the consequence of a heater problem; higher boiler firing is then a response, not the original cause.

4. Validate with related signals

Use multiple independent measurements and compare against operating context before deciding on the failure mechanism.

5. Quantify the performance effect

Translate the confirmed deviation into heat-rate, output, auxiliary-power or efficiency impact using plant-specific validated calculations.

6. Correct and re-baseline

After maintenance or operating correction, verify that the expected relationships recover and update the reference baseline if the plant configuration has legitimately changed.

Important Limitation

Performance penalties are plant-specific.

The source material includes illustrative heat-rate and economic penalties for changes in steam temperature, pressure, excess oxygen, condenser back pressure and heater availability. Those numbers are not universal constants. Actual penalties depend on unit design, load, steam conditions, cooling system, fuel, equipment condition and the calculation method. Public-facing diagnostics should therefore use validated plant-specific curves or calculations rather than copying a generic penalty value.
