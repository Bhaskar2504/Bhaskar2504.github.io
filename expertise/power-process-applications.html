Power & Process Applications

Applying reliability, performance, and analytics methods across thermal power, CCGT, plant auxiliaries, electrical systems, and process assets.

Industrial analytics becomes useful when it respects the physics and operating purpose of the equipment. Pumps, fans, turbines, boilers, electrical systems and process loops all produce data, but the relationships that matter are different for each system.

Engineering principle

The tag does not know what system it belongs to. The engineer must.

A pressure value is just a number until we know whether it is pump suction pressure, turbine exhaust pressure, drum pressure or a common header. Current is not meaningful without knowing which motor, feeder or bus it belongs to. Temperature may indicate heat transfer, bearing friction, cooling performance or simply a change in load. The system boundary gives industrial data its meaning.

Start with the plant system, not the analytics

An asset should not be interpreted in isolation from the process it serves. I begin by defining what enters the system, what leaves it, what drives it, what controls it and what physical constraints govern its behaviour.

PumpConnect suction conditions, discharge system, flow path, hydraulic resistance, motor loading and equipment status.
FanRelate air or gas flow, pressure rise, damper or vane position, system resistance, current and bearing condition.
TurbineConnect steam conditions, load, speed, vibration, temperatures, condenser conditions and control response.
Boiler / HRSGConnect fuel or heat input, air/flue-gas path, steam/water circuit, heat transfer, draft and efficiency.
Electrical equipmentRespect the actual electrical boundary: feeder, motor, bus, transformer or generator—not whichever signal happens to be available.

Application areas

Rotating equipment

Pumps, fans, compressors and turbines: vibration, hydraulic or aerodynamic behaviour, temperatures, load and process response.

Thermal systems

Boilers, HRSGs, heat exchangers and steam cycles: heat transfer, efficiency, temperature approach and operating losses.

Electrical systems

Motors, generators and plant electrical distribution: current, voltage, power, loading and equipment-specific electrical behaviour.

Process assets

Valves, cooling loops, water systems and other process equipment where flow, pressure, temperature and control response interact.

Rotating equipment: understand both the machine and the process

A rotating machine can be mechanically healthy but operating at a poor process point, or mechanically stressed because the surrounding system has changed. The diagnostic view therefore needs both condition measurements and process measurements.

ConditionVibration, bearing temperature, axial position, speed and lubrication-related measurements.
ProcessFlow, pressure, differential pressure, temperature and valve/damper position.
DriverMotor current, active power, turbine load or other input that represents the work being supplied.
Operating stateRunning, standby, startup, shutdown, parallel operation and changeover context.

For a pump, for example, suction restriction should be evaluated through suction conditions, differential pressure, flow and pump performance. Motor current can support the analysis, but its direction depends on the operating point and system curve; it should not be treated as a universal symptom by itself.

Thermal systems: relationships are the performance signal

Thermal systems are dominated by balances and temperature relationships. A heat exchanger or air preheater can degrade while no single temperature appears extreme. The evidence may instead be a weaker temperature rise, changing gas-side oxygen, increased draft-system effort or deteriorating approach temperature.

01Energy inputFuel, steam, gas or process heat entering the system.
02Transfer mechanismHeat-transfer surface, flow path and effective temperature difference.
03Useful outputSteam generation, heated air, cooled fluid or other desired thermal result.
04Loss indicatorsExcess oxygen, stack temperature, leakage, pressure drop or other loss mechanisms.
05Performance KPIEfficiency, heat-rate contribution, approach temperature or normalized loss.

Electrical systems: measure at the correct boundary

Electrical calculations become misleading when measurements from one electrical boundary are applied to another. A busbar power factor represents the combined load connected to that bus; it is not automatically the power factor of an individual motor. If individual active power is unavailable, motor power estimation should use signals and assumptions that actually belong to that motor or feeder, with the limitation stated clearly.

Electrical topology matters as much as the formula.

Before calculating power, current or power factor, identify which source is feeding the connected bus, which feeder supplies the motor, and whether the measurement is individual or common to several loads.

Process systems: control response can look like equipment degradation

Valves, bypasses, cooling loops and control systems can redistribute flow or change pressure and temperature without a mechanical failure. A diagnostic model must therefore understand both the process response and the control action that caused it. Otherwise a normal control movement can be mistaken for degradation—or a valve problem can be mistaken for a pump or sensor problem.

Flow path

Which valves, bypasses, headers and parallel paths can change the measured behaviour?

Control mode

Is the loop in automatic, manual, cascade or another state that changes expected response?

Redundancy

Are pumps, fans, trains or cooling paths operating in parallel or changing duty?

Instrumentation

Do related measurements support the process story, or does one sensor disagree with the rest?

The common engineering workflow across assets

01Define boundaryAsset, connected process and electrical interfaces.
02Map physicsInputs, outputs, balances, constraints and expected relationships.
03Select signalsMeasurements that genuinely represent the system.
04Derive KPIsCalculate physically meaningful performance and condition indicators.
05Compare behaviourExpected versus actual under comparable operating context.
06Diagnose & actTranslate the deviation into an engineering investigation or decision.

What domain knowledge changes

  • It determines which tags actually belong in the model.
  • It prevents common-header or bus measurements from being incorrectly assigned to individual equipment.
  • It identifies useful calculated parameters and avoids meaningless ratios.
  • It separates normal operating transitions from genuine degradation.
  • It constrains failure hypotheses to mechanisms that are physically plausible.
  • It makes the output explainable in the language used by plant engineers.
