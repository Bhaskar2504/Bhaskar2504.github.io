Asset Performance Analytics

Building engineering KPIs, calculated parameters, asset templates, and operating-context logic to improve reliability and performance.

Asset performance analytics turns raw measurements into engineering information. The aim is not to create more dashboards, but to create parameters and views that help engineers understand how efficiently, reliably and consistently an asset is operating.

Illustrative engineering scenario

Ten raw tags can still leave the real question unanswered.

A pump dashboard may show suction pressure, discharge pressure, flow, current, temperature and valve position. Every number is useful, but an engineer may still be asking a simpler question: is the pump doing more work to deliver less useful output? That question is often easier to answer with derived quantities—differential pressure, normalized flow, specific power, temperature rise or operating-point comparisons—than by staring at raw tags one by one.

Raw measurements are the beginning, not the final engineering view

Industrial historians are organized around measurements. Engineering decisions are usually organized around relationships. Asset performance analytics bridges that gap by turning measurements into physically meaningful indicators.

Raw tagA direct measurement such as pressure, flow, current, temperature, vibration or valve position.
Calculated parameterA derived quantity such as differential pressure, temperature rise, efficiency, specific consumption or normalized load.
KPIA parameter selected because it says something important about condition, efficiency, operating margin or business-relevant performance.
ContextThe operating state that determines whether the KPI should be compared, filtered, normalized or ignored.

The analytics stack I use

01MeasurementsValidate tags, units, ranges and sensor quality.
02Calculated valuesCombine measurements into engineering quantities.
03ContextCondition the calculation on load, status, operating mode and valid data.
04Expected behaviourUnderstand how the KPI should respond to operating drivers.
05DeviationCompare actual performance with expected or reference behaviour.
06DecisionTranslate the deviation into maintenance, performance or operating action.

Examples of useful engineering transformation

The exact calculations depend on the asset and available instrumentation. The key principle is to derive variables that reflect the physics of the system rather than creating ratios simply because the data exists.

Pressure relationshipSuction, discharge and differential pressure can reveal more about hydraulic behaviour than either pressure alone.
Temperature relationshipInlet/outlet temperature, temperature rise or approach temperature can expose heat-transfer and cooling behaviour.
Specific consumptionPower or resource consumption normalized by useful output can show deterioration that raw consumption cannot.
Normalized performanceComparing similar operating points helps separate genuine degradation from simple load variation.
Operating marginDistance from a physical, design or process constraint can be more useful than the absolute value itself.

Asset templates: standardize without pretending every asset is identical

Reusable asset templates are valuable because they create a common language for similar equipment. A pump template, for example, can define expected tags, calculated parameters, failure-mode categories and visualizations. But a template should remain configurable because real installations differ in instrumentation, control philosophy, duty and operating envelope.

Common structure

Shared naming, calculations and KPI definitions across similar equipment.

Asset-specific mapping

Each physical asset is connected to the correct historian signals and status logic.

Reusable validation

Standard checks reduce configuration errors while preserving engineering review.

Scalable improvement

Improvements to the template can be propagated across many assets more consistently.

Why operating context changes everything

The same KPI can mean different things under different conditions. A pump’s current changes with flow and hydraulic resistance. Turbine temperatures respond to load and steam conditions. Fan current responds to flow, damper position and system resistance. A meaningful analytics layer therefore asks, “Compared with what operating condition?”

Context is not an optional filter added after the calculation.

It is part of the engineering definition of the calculation itself. Shutdowns, starts, low-load operation, changeovers and parallel operation should be handled deliberately.

Reliability and performance should not live in separate worlds

An asset can remain available while becoming less efficient, and performance deterioration can sometimes be an early sign of developing equipment problems. Combining condition indicators with performance KPIs creates a more complete view than treating maintenance and efficiency as independent disciplines.

Reliability view

Is the equipment developing a condition that increases failure risk?

Performance view

Is the equipment delivering the expected output for the energy or process input?

Operational view

Is the system being operated inside an appropriate and efficient window?

Data-quality view

Can the measurements themselves be trusted enough to support the conclusion?

What good asset analytics should achieve

  • Reduce dependence on isolated static limits.
  • Make the physical meaning of deviations easier to understand.
  • Create consistent calculations across similar equipment.
  • Preserve asset-specific instrumentation and operating differences.
  • Connect condition, efficiency and operating context in one engineering view.
  • Make data quality visible rather than hiding it beneath dashboards.
