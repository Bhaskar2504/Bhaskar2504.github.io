Engineering-Driven AI Diagnostics

Combining equipment behaviour, failure mechanisms, process relationships, and analytics to improve diagnosis beyond black-box anomaly detection.

Detecting that something is unusual is only the first step. The harder question is why it is unusual. Engineering-driven diagnostics uses the physical relationships around an asset to turn a statistical deviation into a focused engineering investigation.

Illustrative engineering scenario

One signal says “problem.” The rest of the machine says “look again.”

Imagine one winding-temperature measurement suddenly rises while electrical loading, cooling conditions and peer temperature measurements remain stable. A purely threshold-based interpretation may call this overheating. A diagnostic interpretation asks whether the rest of the physical system supports that story. When the evidence disagrees, an instrumentation issue becomes a credible hypothesis that should be verified before the machine itself is blamed.

An anomaly is evidence, not a root cause

A model may show that vibration, temperature, pressure or current has departed from expected behaviour. That does not prove a specific failure mode. Diagnosis requires a second layer of reasoning: what physical mechanism could create this combination of changes?

ObservationWhat changed, when did it change, and was the change persistent?
ContextWhat else was happening in the plant at the same time—load change, startup, valve movement, parallel operation, ambient change?
MechanismWhich mechanical, hydraulic, thermal, electrical, control or instrumentation mechanism could explain the pattern?
ContradictionWhich measurements do not fit the hypothesis? A strong diagnosis actively searches for evidence against itself.

The diagnostic chain

01DetectSurface a credible multivariable deviation.
02SequenceIdentify which signals moved first and how the pattern developed.
03RelateCompare the signals with expected equipment and process relationships.
04HypothesizeGenerate plausible failure mechanisms, not just labels.
05ChallengeLook for contradicting measurements and alternative explanations.
06VerifyUse engineering checks, field evidence and inspection to confirm the cause.

Why relationships carry more information than isolated tags

Industrial equipment operates as a connected physical system. If a suction restriction develops in a pump system, the effect can appear through suction conditions, differential pressure, flow and discharge performance. If heat transfer deteriorates, temperatures, oxygen levels and draft-system loading can change together. If a sensor drifts, the measured value may separate from peer signals that should normally agree with it.

MechanicalVibration, axial movement, bearing temperatures and their relationship with speed or load.
HydraulicFlow, suction/discharge pressure, differential pressure, valve position and pump operating response.
ThermalTemperature approach, inlet/outlet relationships, heat-transfer behaviour and cooling conditions.
ElectricalCurrent, voltage, active power, reactive behaviour and equipment loading.
InstrumentationAgreement between redundant, peer or physically related measurements.

What AI contributes—and what it should not pretend to do

AI is useful for learning expected behaviour, ranking deviations, finding multivariable patterns and reviewing more data than a human can continuously inspect. But a high anomaly score does not automatically identify the physical cause. Engineering reasoning is still required to distinguish between degradation, process change, control action, measurement error and a genuine failure mechanism.

The goal is not to replace engineering judgment with a model score.

The goal is to use analytics to focus engineering attention on patterns that are difficult to see manually, then use physics and evidence to decide what those patterns mean.

A practical way to rank hypotheses

When several causes are possible, I prefer a structured diagnostic view rather than a single confident label.

Consistency

Does the proposed cause explain the direction and timing of all major signal changes?

Coverage

Does it explain the full pattern or only one measurement?

Contradictions

Are there signals that should have changed if the hypothesis were true but did not?

Verification path

Can the hypothesis be tested through inspection, another measurement, operating comparison or maintenance evidence?

What good diagnostics should look like

  • Evidence-first: show what changed before naming a cause.
  • Mechanism-based: connect the evidence to physical failure mechanisms.
  • Probabilistic in language: use “plausible,” “consistent with,” or “needs verification” when the evidence is not conclusive.
  • Context-aware: distinguish degradation from startup, shutdown, duty change and process disturbances.
  • Actionable: point to what an engineer should check next.
