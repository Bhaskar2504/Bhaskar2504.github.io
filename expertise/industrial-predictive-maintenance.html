Industrial Predictive Maintenance

Using industrial data, engineering knowledge, and AI to identify developing equipment problems before they become failures.

Predictive maintenance is most useful when it does more than detect an unusual signal. The objective is to recognize a developing change early enough for engineers to understand it, validate it and decide what action is justified.

Illustrative engineering scenario

The machine is still inside its alarm limits. Is everything really normal?

Consider a continuously operating pump. Vibration, bearing temperature, motor current and discharge pressure all remain inside their individual alarm limits. Yet the vibration response to load has slowly become stronger, the bearing temperature takes longer to settle after a load change, and the relationship between current and delivered flow is no longer as repeatable as before. No single tag is alarming. The behaviour of the asset, however, is changing. That is the space where predictive maintenance adds value.

What predictive maintenance should actually answer

A useful predictive-maintenance system should progressively answer four different questions. Treating them as the same question is one of the main reasons industrial monitoring becomes noisy or untrusted.

Is something changing?

Detect a persistent departure from the asset’s expected behaviour rather than reacting to every small fluctuation.

Is the change credible?

Check data quality, operating state, maintenance activity, control actions and other explanations before treating the deviation as degradation.

What could explain it?

Relate the pattern to plausible mechanical, hydraulic, thermal, electrical, control or instrumentation mechanisms.

What should be done?

Create enough engineering confidence to inspect, test, trend, plan maintenance or continue monitoring with a defined reason.

Why static alarms are not enough

Traditional alarm limits remain essential for protection and operations, but they answer a different question: has a measurement reached a predefined limit? Predictive monitoring asks whether the way the asset behaves has changed before that limit is reached.

Absolute valueA vibration value may still be acceptable, but its sensitivity to load or speed may be increasing.
RelationshipPressure, flow and current may each look reasonable while the relationship between them becomes inconsistent with historical operation.
PersistenceA one-point spike may be noise. A structured deviation that persists across several operating periods deserves more attention.
ContextThe same temperature or current can mean different things during startup, baseload operation, low load, changeover or shutdown.

The end-to-end engineering workflow

01Understand the assetPurpose, design, operating modes, protections and common failure mechanisms.
02Validate the dataUnits, ranges, missing values, flat lines, timing, sensor quality and tag mapping.
03Learn normal behaviourHow the asset responds to load, speed, flow, ambient and process conditions.
04Detect deviationPersistent multivariable change rather than isolated movement.
05DiagnoseCompare the pattern with physical mechanisms and contradicting evidence.
06Act and learnInspect, validate, plan maintenance and feed the outcome back into monitoring.

What signals matter?

The useful signal set depends on the asset. The aim is not to map every available tag; it is to capture both the condition of the equipment and the operating forces acting on it.

Mechanical responseVibration, bearing temperature, axial position, speed and other condition measurements.
Hydraulic / processSuction and discharge pressure, differential pressure, flow, valve position and process demand.
Electrical loadingCurrent, active power, voltage, power factor and other feeder-specific electrical quantities when available.
Thermal behaviourInlet/outlet temperatures, temperature rise, cooling conditions and heat-transfer indicators.
Operating contextLoad, speed, equipment status, parallel operation, startup/shutdown state and control mode.

The difficult part: separating degradation from normal plant behaviour

Industrial time-series data is rarely clean and stationary. Pumps change duty. Units ramp. Valves move. Instruments drift. Equipment is shut down for maintenance. A model that ignores these operating realities can generate many mathematically valid but operationally useless anomalies.

A good predictive alert is not simply “the model error is high.”

It should survive basic engineering checks: the asset is operating, the data is credible, the deviation is persistent, the pattern is unusual for the current operating context, and there is a plausible reason for an engineer to investigate it.

What good implementation looks like

  • Early enough to act: detection should create useful engineering time, not simply announce that the equipment is already in distress.
  • Explainable enough to investigate: engineers should be able to see which variables changed and how they relate to the operating condition.
  • Context-aware: startups, shutdowns, low-load operation, equipment changeovers and maintenance states must be handled explicitly.
  • Failure-mode aware: anomaly detection should support, not replace, engineering diagnosis.
  • Feedback-driven: inspection findings and plant feedback should improve future interpretation.
