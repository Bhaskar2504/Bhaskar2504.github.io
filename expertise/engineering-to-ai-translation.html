Engineering-to-AI Translation

Converting plant-domain problems into clear requirements that AI, ML, product, and software teams can implement.

Industrial AI projects often fail at the boundary between domain knowledge and implementation. Engineers describe the plant problem in operational language, while data science and software teams need measurable inputs, logic, constraints and expected outputs. Translation connects those two worlds.

Illustrative translation problem

“The pump is behaving strangely” is an engineering observation—not yet an implementable requirement.

An operator may know that a pump is no longer behaving the way it normally does after a changeover. That statement contains valuable domain knowledge, but a development team still needs to know which signals define the pump state, how the individual flow is determined, what counts as normal changeover behaviour, how long the transition may last, which operating states should be excluded, and what output the user expects. Good translation preserves the engineering meaning while making every assumption explicit.

Start with the decision, not the algorithm

The most important first question is not “Which AI model should we use?” It is “What decision are we trying to improve?” A clear engineering question naturally determines the data, model, logic and user experience that follow.

Early detectionWe need to identify a developing change before a conventional alarm or failure point.
DiagnosisWe need to explain which physical mechanisms are consistent with the observed pattern.
PerformanceWe need to quantify efficiency, loss, margin or deviation from expected operation.
ForecastingWe need to estimate a future process or equipment quantity with known inputs and operating context.
Decision supportWe need to compare alternatives or calculate the consequence of changing selected inputs.

The translation chain

01Plant questionWhat is the engineer trying to understand or decide?
02System boundaryWhich asset, upstream/downstream systems and operating states matter?
03Engineering variablesWhich raw tags, statuses and derived quantities describe the problem?
04Expected behaviourWhat should happen physically when the operating drivers change?
05Logic / analyticsWhat calculation, model or rule converts inputs into useful evidence?
06User outputWhat should the user see, understand and do next?

From domain language to technical specification

A strong requirement removes ambiguity without stripping away engineering context. I normally break the problem into a small set of technical questions.

What is the asset?

Define equipment hierarchy, duty, boundaries, connected systems and relevant operating states.

What are the inputs?

List the source measurements, statuses, calculations, units and expected data availability.

What is the physics?

Describe expected direction, dependency, cause-and-effect relationship and constraints.

What is the output?

Define whether the user needs a KPI, anomaly, diagnosis, forecast, ranking, calculation or explanation.

When is it valid?

Specify startup, shutdown, standby, low-load, parallel-operation and invalid-data rules.

How will we accept it?

Define test scenarios and what correct behaviour should look like during validation and UAT.

Calculated tags are a translation problem too

Many industrial requirements look simple until the equation is connected to real data. A calculation must define every variable, source, unit, operating condition and fallback rule. For example, estimating motor power is not just a formula—it depends on whether active power is measured, whether the power factor belongs to the individual motor or only to a common bus, and whether voltage and current are available at the correct electrical boundary.

The equation is only one part of the requirement.

The implementation also needs the source hierarchy, validity rules, units, equipment status and what to do when a preferred signal is unavailable.

Failure-mode logic needs the same discipline

A failure mode should describe a physical condition or mechanism—not simply restate the tags that are high or low. Translating a diagnostic requirement therefore requires moving from symptoms to engineering meaning.

Weak requirement“Current low + voltage low + power low.” This is only a list of observed conditions.
Better requirementIdentify the equipment-level electrical or process condition that could create the combination, then specify the supporting and contradicting evidence.
ValidationConfirm whether the same pattern could arise during shutdown, load reduction, instrumentation failure or other normal states.

Why cross-functional communication is a technical skill

Different teams naturally optimize for different things. Plant engineers focus on physical correctness and operational risk. Data scientists focus on model behaviour. Product teams focus on workflows. Software teams focus on deterministic logic, dependencies and edge cases. Customer-success and implementation teams focus on adoption and practical delivery. The translation role ensures that the same requirement means the same thing to all of them.

EngineeringPhysics, failure mechanisms, operating context, constraints and acceptance.
Data scienceFeatures, target behaviour, training context, error, uncertainty and model outputs.
ProductUser journey, visibility, configuration, interpretation and workflow.
SoftwareData dependencies, logic, edge cases, states, APIs and implementation rules.
OperationsWhat the result means at the plant and what action is realistically possible.

What good translation looks like

  • The engineering problem can be understood without knowing the chosen algorithm.
  • Every required signal and calculated variable has a defined meaning and unit.
  • Operating-state rules are explicit rather than assumed.
  • Physics and expected behaviour are documented before validation begins.
  • Acceptance criteria describe real scenarios, not only software functionality.
  • The final output answers a user question rather than simply exposing model internals.
