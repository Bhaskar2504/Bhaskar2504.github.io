The Air Preheater That Quietly Changed the Boiler

No single parameter looked dramatic. The story emerged only when temperature, oxygen and draft demand were read together.

An air preheater sits at an important thermal boundary. It recovers heat from flue gas and transfers it to the combustion-air side. When that exchange works well, the downstream temperatures, oxygen behavior and fan loading tend to follow familiar patterns.

The first clues

Imagine primary or secondary air outlet temperature beginning to fall for a similar operating condition. At the same time, flue-gas oxygen trends upward and the induced-draft system starts working harder than expected.

Temperature

Useful heat recovery is weakening.

Oxygen

Additional air may be entering the gas path.

Draft demand

The fan may be handling more gas volume than before.

Why the three signals belong together

A lower air outlet temperature alone could be blamed on load, ambient condition or heat-transfer variation. Higher oxygen alone could be blamed on combustion control. Higher fan loading alone could look like a fan issue. When all three move consistently, a common physical mechanism becomes more credible.

What the thermodynamics may suggest

Seal deterioration or air ingress across the preheater is one plausible explanation. Air entering the flue-gas path changes gas mass flow, alters oxygen concentration and weakens effective heat recovery. The fan then has to move the additional gas volume through the downstream path.

If it progresses

The boiler pays twice

Heat recovery can deteriorate while draft power rises, creating both a thermal-efficiency penalty and additional auxiliary loading.

What early detection buys

A planned repair opportunity

Engineers can inspect seals, leakage indicators and heat-transfer performance before the condition becomes a larger operating constraint.

Thermal degradation often appears first as a disagreement between heat transfer, gas chemistry and fan demand.
